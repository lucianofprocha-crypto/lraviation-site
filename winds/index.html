Wind barbs — build one

The shaft points to where the wind comes from (true north). Feathers sit on the low-pressure side of the flow: for a westerly they point poleward — towards the north in the NH, towards the south in the SH (mirror image).

WAFS wind / temperature charts

  • Producers: WAFC London and WAFC Washington, from the same global model data used by flight-planning systems (GRIB2).
  • Levels (charts): FL050, 100, 140, 180, 240, 270, 300, 320, 340, 360, 390, 410, 450 and 530 (varies by provider). The gridded data behind them is what your flight plan uses.
  • Validity: fixed-time forecasts — 00, 06, 12, 18 UTC. Use the chart closest to the time you will be at that point.
  • Grid: one barb per grid point (typically every 5° on printed charts). Direction is true; speed in knots.
  • Temperature: whole °C, negative unless marked “+” (e.g. 56 = −56 °C). Compare with ISA to get the ISA deviation for performance.
  • Isotachs / shading (on some versions) show speed bands — the jet core is the band with the highest speeds.

Rules

  • DDss(±TT): direction in tens of degrees true, speed in knots, temperature °C.
  • 9900 = light and variable (less than 5 kt).
  • Speed 100–199 kt: 50 is added to the direction and 100 subtracted from the speed. Direction digits 51–86 therefore mean 100 kt+: 7319 → 230° / 119 kt.
  • 7799 (direction +50, speed “99”) = 199 kt or more.
  • Above 24,000 ft the minus sign is omitted — all temperatures there are negative.
  • No wind is forecast within 1,500 ft of station elevation, and no temperature at 3,000 ft or within 2,500 ft of station elevation.

Why the winds blow that way

  • Geostrophic wind: away from the equator, the pressure-gradient force is balanced by Coriolis — the wind blows parallel to the isobars/contours, faster where they are packed.
  • Buys Ballot: back to the wind, low pressure is on the left in the NH and on the right in the SH. Lows turn anticlockwise in the NH and clockwise in the SH; highs the opposite.
  • Thermal wind: the air is colder towards the poles, so pressure surfaces slope down poleward more and more with height → westerlies increase with height up to the tropopause (jet cores), then decrease above.
  • Three-cell circulation: surface trade winds blow from the subtropical highs to the ITCZ (NE trades in the NH, SE trades in the SH). Aloft the flow returns poleward and is turned by Coriolis into the subtropical westerly jet at the edge of the Hadley cell (~25–35°).
  • Near the equator Coriolis vanishes: winds aloft are light and mostly easterly, strongest in the summer hemisphere (monsoon anticyclones aloft).
  • The winter hemisphere has the larger pole–equator temperature contrast → stronger jets, closer to the equator. The whole pattern follows the sun: north in July, south in January.

Transition seasons (Mar–May, Sep–Nov) sit in between; the SH jet over southern Brazil already strengthens in Apr–May, the NH jet is still present in Apr — so late autumn (SH) is often the worst time for northbound headwinds to the USA.

Wind triangle

Mach → TAS · ISA deviation

TAS = 661.47 × M × √(T / 288.15), T in kelvin (static air temperature). Use the TAS result in the wind triangle above.

Equal-time point (ETP)

ETP from departure = D × GSreturn / (GScontinue + GSreturn). Enter + for tailwind, − for headwind (for engine-out / depressurisation ETPs use the TAS and level of that scenario). A tailwind towards the destination moves the ETP back towards departure.
