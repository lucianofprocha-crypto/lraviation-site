What a SIGWX chart is

A fixed-time forecast of significant en-route weather produced by the two World Area Forecast Centres (WAFC London and WAFC Washington). It is a snapshot for the validity time printed on the chart, not a forecast for a period. Legacy T+24 charts are issued for 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC. ICAO: the forecast may be used from 3 h before to 3 h after the validity time.

Heights are flight levels (hundreds of feet), speeds in knots. XXX = the level is outside the chart's layer (above the top or below the base). Only moderate and severe phenomena are shown — light turbulence or icing never appears.

Reading method — where to look

Tap a number on the chart or a step below — the matching area is highlighted. Practice SWH chart, invented data.

1

Header box. Check the chart type (SWH/SWM), the layer (e.g. FL250–600), the validity date/time and the issuing WAFC. A chart may be used from 3 h before to 3 h after its validity time — use the chart whose validity is closest to the time you are over each part of the route.

2

Standing notes. Read the fixed text: units, and the note that “CB implies TS, GR (hail), moderate or severe turbulence and icing” — those hazards are therefore not drawn separately around CB areas. Also “CHECK SIGMET, ADVISORIES FOR TC AND VA, AND ASHTAM AND NOTAM FOR VA” and, when a radioactive release is shown, “CHECK SIGMET AND NOTAM FOR RDOACT CLD”.

3

Jet streams. Thick arrowed lines where wind ≥ 80 kt. At the wind flag read the maximum speed and its FL; if ≥ 120 kt a pair of levels gives the depth of the ≥ 80 kt layer. Double bars across the jet (change bars) mark a speed change of 20 kt or a core-height change of 3,000 ft. Read the direction from the arrowhead — jets are not always westerly. Flags sit on the left of the core looking downwind in the NH, on the right in the SH. Think headwind/tailwind and CAT.

4

Cumulonimbus. Scalloped outlines labelled with amount (OCNL / FRQ) and top over base (e.g. 470 over XXX). Compare the top with your cruise level — an aircraft at FL450 does not overfly a CB area topping FL470.

5

Turbulence. Heavy dashed outlines, usually numbered, with the symbol (single hat = MOD, double hat = SEV) and top/base. Since 2025 this covers CAT, in-cloud and orographic turbulence (EDR-based).

6

Icing (SWM). (not on this SWH chart) MOD/SEV icing areas with top/base — relevant for climb and descent through FL100–FL250.

7

Tropopause. Thin dashed contours labelled FL (e.g. FL300). Low tropopause = colder air mass, often with jets and CAT nearby; tropopause breaks and steep slopes are CAT areas.

8

Other hazards. Volcano (name + position), radioactive release, tropical cyclone (name), sandstorm/duststorm. SIGWX only shows the location — get details from VA advisories, SIGMET, NOTAM and TC advisories.

Features

    Chart questions

    Jet stream patterns — hemisphere, season, latitude

    Schematic globe — jets drawn on the visible hemisphere. Line thickness = relative strength; dashed = weak / intermittent.

    Pole-to-pole cross-section: tropopause (dashed purple) and jet cores just below the tropopause breaks.

    Polar front jetSubtropical jetTropical easterly jetTropopause

    The main jets

    JetTypical latitudeTypical levelDirectionCharacter
    Polar front jet (PFJ)~40–60° (30–70°)FL300–350WesterlyStrongly meandering, broken into segments; tied to mid-latitude fronts and lows
    Subtropical jet (STJ)~25–35°FL350–400WesterlyMore continuous and zonal; poleward edge of the Hadley cell
    Tropical easterly jet (TEJ)~5–20°NFL450–500EasterlyNorthern summer only (Jun–Sep), Asian monsoon: India → Arabia → Africa
    Both westerly jets sit just below the tropopause breaks: the PFJ between the low polar and the mid-latitude tropopause, the STJ between the mid-latitude and the high tropical tropopause — so the STJ is higher than the PFJ.

    Season — both hemispheres

    • Winter: larger pole–equator temperature contrast → jets stronger (PFJ often 150–200 kt+) and displaced towards the equator (PFJ ~35–45°).
    • Summer: jets weaker and shifted poleward (PFJ ~50–60°); in the NH the STJ can almost disappear in places.
    • Autumn / spring: transition — strengthening and moving equatorward in autumn, weakening and moving poleward in spring.
    • Rule of thumb: the winter hemisphere has the strongest jets, closest to the equator.

    Northern vs southern hemisphere

    • NH: more land and mountains (Rockies, Himalaya) → jets more wavy and variable. Strongest jets off the east coasts: Japan / W Pacific (STJ can exceed 200 kt in winter) and eastern USA / N Atlantic.
    • SH: mostly ocean → jets more zonal and more constant through the year; persistent STJ over Australia, southern Africa and southern Brazil / northern Argentina in winter (~25–35°S).
    • In both hemispheres the cold (poleward) side is the low-pressure side: that is where the flags are drawn (left in NH, right in SH, looking downwind) and where CAT is usually strongest, just below the core.

    Other patterns

    • Jet merging: where PFJ and STJ come together (Japan, eastern USA) speeds add up — CAT zone.
    • Troughs and ridges: the jet follows the waves; in deep troughs it can blow northward, southward or even westward. Sharp curvature = CAT.
    • Entrance / exit regions: acceleration and deceleration along the axis (change bars) also produce turbulence.
    • Not on SWH: African easterly jet (~FL100–150, northern summer, W Africa), polar night jet (stratosphere, winter, far above FL600), low-level jets.
    N Atlantic ops: in winter the N Atlantic PFJ is strong and further south — large tailwind eastbound, strong headwind westbound, NAT tracks move the most. In summer it weakens and moves north; the STJ lies over N Africa and the Mediterranean.

    Figures are typical values for study — the actual jet on any day is the one on the chart.

    New IWXXM SIGWX data

    • One global dataset FL100 – FL600 (no separate SWH/SWM).
    • Validity T+6 to T+48 every 3 hours — used by flight planning / EFB systems to draw charts for any of these times.
    • CB base is not included (it was usually “XXX” anyway).
